# Niyora — PMS at work content bank (v1, research-backed)

Source science for any "PMS and the workplace" copy. Answers four questions:
what we're **sure** of, what we **assume** but nobody has studied, what's a
**myth**, and how much of it can be **managed or treated**.

**Voice** (for shipped card copy only, not this ledger): neutrally warm, like a
smart Californian friend who knows the science. Contractions, affirmative, no em
dashes, no "this will help" promises, never "it's normal." See
[[niyora-pms-understand-content]].

**Provenance:** every claim below went through a 3-skeptic adversarial
verification pass with live literature search (2026-07-11), the same bar as the
Understand shelf and the [[niyora-emotion-bank]]. The raw harvest lives in the
research cockpit (Europe PMC → DuckDB), "Workplace / productivity" (57 papers)
and "Occupational" (53 papers) lanes, all still uncurated. Skeptics attacked for
phase-conflation (menstrual bleed vs premenstrual/luteal), reverse causation,
and single-country/self-report bias.

## Confidence tags (same convention as the emotion bank)

- **[VERIFIED]** — survived 3/0, meta-analytic or high-certainty source. Ship-safe.
- **[MEDIUM]** — real but single-study, correlational, or caveated. State softly, never as cause.
- **[GAP]** — promising but did not clear this pass. Needs a second look before it becomes copy.
- **[DO NOT USE]** — refuted, or an over-specific claim with no evidence. Safe only as a *wrong* quiz option, never as a fact.

The governing honesty rule for this topic: **none of the effect findings are
causal, and most "premenstrual" workplace instruments actually measure the whole
cycle.** Say "linked to," never "causes." Say "menstrual symptoms" unless the
study genuinely isolated the luteal phase.

## 1. Sure — backed by science (what affects women at work)

### More sick leave and job loss over time
**[MEDIUM]** In a Swedish prospective cohort of 15,857 employed women followed
about 9 years, those with probable premenstrual disorders had roughly 40 percent
higher risk of sick leave and 27 percent higher risk of unemployment. This is the
strongest finding in the set because the work outcomes came from national
registers, not self-report, and the link held even in women without a depression
or anxiety diagnosis.
*Caveat (why MEDIUM not VERIFIED): not causal (a moderately strong unmeasured
confounder, E-value 1.86, would null the unemployment result); reflects Sweden's
specific welfare system and may not transfer; the "PMD" group was defined partly
by SSRI/contraceptive proxies that can leak in unrelated cases.*
*Source: Swedish LifeGene cohort, BMJ Mental Health 2025 — https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12258349/*

### At work, mood and focus drag on productivity more than pain does
**[MEDIUM]** Among 1,988 Japanese working women, the symptoms tied most to
presenteeism (being at work but underperforming) were low mood, poor
concentration, and behaviour change. Physical pain correlated more weakly. The
severe-symptom group had about 30 percent higher overall work impairment.
*Caveat: the instrument (Menstrual Distress Questionnaire) measures the whole
cycle, so this is a **menstrual**-symptom finding, not strictly premenstrual.
"More than pain," never "not pain" (a sister 32,748-woman cohort did find
abdominal pain significant). Cross-sectional, app-recruited, self-reported, Japan
only.*
*Source: "Beyond Pain," BJOG 2026 — https://obgyn.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/1471-0528.70178*

### Reduced productivity and attendance, across many studies
**[MEDIUM]** A 2025 systematic review (37 studies, 72,567 women; 15 on PMS
specifically) found moderate-to-severe PMS consistently linked to more
absenteeism and lower productivity, with odds ratios landing roughly between 2
and 6 across studies.
*Caveat: heterogeneity was too high to pool, so that range is a spread across
weak, mostly cross-sectional, self-defined-PMS studies, **not** a single pooled
effect. Never present "2.4–5.2 / 3.1–6.0" as one summary number.*
*Source: systematic review, Heliyon 2025 — https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2405844025018559*

### The through-line
The symptoms with the best workplace evidence are the **emotional** ones (low
mood, anxiety), showing up mainly as presenteeism rather than days off. Pain and
"fog" are weaker or contested (see sections 2 and 3).

## 2. How much can be managed or treated

Encouragingly, the mood symptoms that most affect work are also the most
treatable. Ordered by evidence strength.

### GnRH analogues — strong effect, specialist and short-term only
**[VERIFIED]** The only high-certainty result in this batch. GnRH agonists
strongly reduce overall PMS symptoms (SMD about -1.23). But they induce a
menopause-like state with bone-loss risk, so they're reserved for short-term or
treatment-resistant severe cases, not everyday management.
*Source: Cochrane 2025, 11 RCTs — https://www.cochranelibrary.com/cdsr/doi/10.1002/14651858.CD011330.pub2/full*

### SSRIs — modest, prescription, with a work-relevant catch
**[MEDIUM]** Across 34 RCTs, SSRIs modestly reduce PMS/PMDD symptoms (SMD about
-0.57, moderate certainty), and work a bit better taken continuously than
luteal-only. **Honesty landmine for a work-focused app:** a confirmed side effect
is decreased concentration and drowsiness (odds roughly tripled, OR 3.26). If a
piece ever pairs "SSRIs help" with "perform better at work," that side effect has
to be named in the same breath. Also: 68 percent of the trials were
pharma-funded, with suspected publication bias, which is why it's moderate not
high certainty. A more-severe-case option, not a first move for mild PMS.
*Source: Cochrane 2024, 34 RCTs — https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11323276/*

### Exercise — low-risk, helps mood, fatigue, pain
**[MEDIUM]** Across 20 trials, regular yoga or aerobic exercise (about 3+
sessions a week for 9–12 weeks in these studies) was linked to better mood,
fatigue, and pain in PMS. Near-zero harm, so it survives despite soft evidence.
*Caveat: exercise trials can't be blinded, outcomes are self-reported, and the
review itself flagged publication bias, so the true effect is likely smaller than
reported. The specific dose is a post-hoc observation, so "in these trials," not
"you must."*
*Source: systematic review/meta-analysis, BMC Women's Health 2026 — https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12874746/*

### Relaxation / progressive muscle relaxation — promising, not proven
**[GAP]** Two small trials (N=68 and N=106) suggest web-based relaxation may help
women feel calmer and cope better, one reporting improved occupational
performance. But both are tiny, single-center, unblinded on outcomes, with short
or no follow-up, and one used a waitlist control (which inflates any effect
versus doing nothing). Promising and low-risk to try, but not a proven treatment
yet, and it needs a second research pass before it becomes copy.
*Do-not-use note: the N=106 trial's "violence tendencies" outcome rests on one
self-report scale. Drop that framing entirely; it's thin and reputationally
hazardous for a women's-health app.*
*Source: J Affect Disord 2026 (N=68) — https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0165032726001291*

## 3. Assumed but no research (plausible, unstudied — do not ship as fact)

### Irritation aimed at a colleague
**[DO NOT USE]** There is no workplace study on premenstrual irritation directed
at coworkers. Irritability as a symptom is real; irritability *aimed at a person*
is an unsupported leap, and the partner-directed version was already refuted (see
section 4).

### Feeling unsafe toward a specific person at work
**[DO NOT USE]** No research establishes this. Qualitative work shows some women
find social interaction harder premenstrually, but "feels unsafe toward a
colleague" is invented specificity. (The one paper using "psychological safety"
at work means organizational dignity and facilities, not fear of a person.)

### Brain fog as a real on-the-job cognitive dip
**[DO NOT USE as a performance claim]** No study tests premenstrual cognition
against actual work output, and the cognition evidence itself argues against a
monthly dip (see section 4).

### A bad premenstrual day causing a specific work mistake or decision
**[GAP]** Never studied at the task level. Don't imply it.

## 4. Myths — thought but refuted (safe only as *wrong* quiz options)

### "The irritability is aimed at the people around her / it wrecks relationships"
**[DO NOT USE] (directed-animosity half).** Irritability is not directed hostility
or irrationality (irrationality has never been a listed symptom). The
partner-animosity framing was refuted 0/3 in the prior pass and again here.
*Nuance worth keeping:* relationship *strain* during the luteal phase is real and
documented, but frame it as shared and support-modifiable, never as her
aggression at others.

### "Your focus and memory measurably drop before your period"
**[DO NOT USE].** The largest meta-analysis (102 studies, 3,943 women) found no
robust cyclic shift in objective cognitive performance. The fog is a felt,
subjective experience that doesn't track measured performance. A 2026 study did
find a working-memory and inhibition deficit in high-symptom women, but it was
**trait-like** (present in all cycle phases, uncorrelated with hormone levels),
so it argues *against* a premenstrual cliff, not for one. Do not cite it for a
cyclical-fog narrative.
*Source: PLOS One 2025 meta-analysis — https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0318576*

### "Women with PMS have abnormal hormones"
**[DO NOT USE].** Hormone levels are normal. The difference is a heightened brain
sensitivity to normal cyclical changes (the differential-sensitivity model). Use
the correct, destigmatizing framing instead. Matches [[niyora-pms-understand-content]].

### "PMS is just emotional / not a real condition"
**[DO NOT USE] (as a claim); the affirmative is worth stating.** PMDD is a
recognized diagnosis (DSM-5, 2013; ICD-11, in effect 2022). State that it's
recognized, but don't blur PMS and PMDD (PMDD is the severe subtype).

## Verified facts bank (numbers for other copy)

- Women with premenstrual disorders: ~40% higher sick-leave risk, ~27% higher unemployment risk over ~9 years (Swedish register cohort; associational, single-country).
- At-work productivity loss tracks mood and concentration more than pain (Japanese working women; menstrual, not strictly premenstrual).
- Moderate-severe PMS linked to more absenteeism and lower productivity, odds ratios ~2–6 across studies (not poolable).
- SSRIs: modest symptom relief (SMD ~-0.57, moderate certainty) but roughly triple the odds of decreased concentration/drowsiness; mostly pharma-funded.
- GnRH agonists: strong relief (SMD ~-1.23, high certainty) but short-term/specialist only (bone-loss risk).
- Exercise (yoga/running, ~9–12 wk, 3+/wk): linked to better mood, fatigue, pain; low risk, soft evidence.
- No credible evidence for: colleague-directed irritation, feeling unsafe toward a coworker, a measurable premenstrual cognitive dip, or abnormal hormones.
